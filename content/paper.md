## Abstract

> The decay neutrinos of a multi-TeV muon collider form pencil beams of
> unprecedented intensity and energy, and their interaction with the
> surrounding landscape — where they graze the Earth's surface — is a
> recognized siting constraint. We invert the constraint into an asset. We
> show that an 11 km collider ring fits the Fermilab campus with its
> interaction-point straight on the true-north meridian −88.222972°,
> tilted upward by 15.4 mrad (one LEP of ring-plane inclination). The
> north-going neutrino beam then leaves the ground *inside the laboratory
> fence*, delivering ~10¹⁰ TeV-scale neutrino interactions per tonne-year
> to an on-site experiment before climbing a utility right-of-way into
> federally navigable airspace; the south-going beam dives beneath the
> region's aquifers to a perigee of 789 m and resurfaces 198 km away on
> University of Illinois agricultural land, where its exit strip doubles
> as a second neutrino experiment of ~10⁸ interactions per tonne-year.
> The second interaction point's south beam exits onto the same
> university's airport. Every point at which a beam passes within 500 ft
> of the ground surface lies on federal, utility-easement, or university
> land, and groundwater activation is bounded at two to four orders of
> magnitude below drinking-water limits. The alignment costs ~11% of one
> accelerator ring's circumference and one LEP-scale plane tilt, and
> converts the muon collider's defining radiological liability into two
> shared-science facilities.
