Where does the beam come out of the ground?

The neutrino plume from the collider's interaction-point straight travels in a perfectly straight line while the Earth's surface curves and the terrain rolls. Pick where the collision point sits along the on-site corridor, how deep the detector hall is, and where you want the beam to emerge — the tool computes the upward tilt the straight section needs, how deep the ring ends up, and the height of the beam over the nearest house at the north end of the ComEd corridor (Smith Road crossing, 41.935618 N). Drag the ▲ IP and ● emergence point directly, or use the sliders.

Reading the answer

  • Straight-section tilt is the beam angle above the local horizontal at the IP. For a planar ring this equals the ring-plane pitch; LEP's plane was tilted 14 mrad (0.8°), so values beyond ~15 mrad are flagged as beyond engineering precedent.
  • The beam line is anchored by two constraints: it must pass through the detector hall (fixed latitude, chosen depth) and graze the surface at your chosen emergence point. Earth curvature (R = 6371 km) makes the required angle at the IP larger than the naive rise-over-run by Δs/R ≈ 1.4 mrad per 9 km, and the beam appears as a gentle parabola against the terrain.
  • An upward-tilted north straight tilts the south-going plume down, pushing the southern exit farther away — helpful, see the safety assessment.
  • Once airborne, the neutrino beam keeps climbing relative to the curving ground; the number that matters for the Smith Road houses is the clearance at the corridor's north end. An overhead neutrino beam in open air produces far less dose than a surface-grazing one (showers need hundreds of metres of air to develop — King 1999, §2), but any occupied-airspace / skyshine case still needs its own assessment.
