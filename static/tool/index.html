Launch a beam, see where it comes out

This is a straight-line ray trace over the real elevation profile of the corridor. You set three things about the machine: where the collision point sits (N–S along the on-site corridor), the height (elevation) of the straightaway, and its upward tilt. The neutrino beam then travels in a perfectly straight line while the Earth curves away beneath it and the terrain rolls above it. The tool shows the whole story downstream: the beam's depth where it crosses the detector (whose latitude you choose — anywhere from inside the Fermilab site to 13 km north), the point where it emerges from the ground, how it sits relative to the airspace over private property north of Smith Road (41.935618 N), which rock units it tunnels through, and how many neutrino interaction lengths of matter each beam crosses on its way out of the Earth.

How big is the beam?

Muon decay is isotropic in the muon's rest frame, so boosting by γ = Eμ/mμ gives the classic beamed profile dN/dΩ ∝ (1 + γ²θ²)⁻² — whose on-axis value is exactly the Nγ²/πL² used for the flux above. That makes the whole profile self-similar: every location sees the same shape, scaled by L/γ. Two consequences worth stating precisely: half the neutrinos land inside 1/γ, and because the boost also correlates energy with angle (⟨E⟩ ∝ (1+γ²θ²)⁻¹) while σν ∝ E, the interaction profile goes as (1+γ²θ²)⁻³ — noticeably tighter than the flux: 75 % of events inside 1/γ, 99 % inside 3/γ.

How the spot grows, and what it leaves on the ground

Every containment radius is simply proportional to distance, so on log–log axes the beam is a straight fan: centimetres while it is still on the Fermilab site, metres by the time it reaches Champaign County. What matters for land use is the right-hand panel — where the beam meets the ground it does so at a grazing angle of a few milliradians, and the circular cross-section is stretched along the surface by 1/sin(graze), turning a metres-wide spot into a long, narrow strip.

An earlier revision of this tool treated the far-field spot as kinematic (r = L/γ) with the divergence confined to a ~10⁻⁴-weight halo. MINT (arXiv:2608.02718), decaying muons along the real IMCC lattice, shows otherwise: a drift conserves angles, the final-focus drifts are ~150 m long, and the whole IP-straight plume carries σθ ≈ 0.1–0.2 mrad ≈ 7×(1/γ), washing out the energy–radius prism. The spot table above is the two-component mixture — smeared plume (weight 1−f) plus optional dedicated-drift pencil (weight f) — and the σθ and f sliders drive every rate, spot and exit-strip number on this page. Civic-envelope audit — who is within 500 ft of this beam?

The siting argument of this study is a ±500 ft rule: wherever the beam is within 500 ft (152 m) of the ground surface — above it or below it — the land should belong to an institutional partner (DOE, the ComEd transmission easement the corridor follows, or the University of Illinois). Above 500 ft the beam is in federally navigable airspace (14 CFR 91.119(c)), where no private surface-owner claim reaches; below 500 ft of depth it is beneath every private well, foundation, and utility along the route (Chicago-area deep tunnels are routinely easement-ed at a fraction of that depth). Where both beams leave the Earth

The straight section fires neutrinos both ways. The north beam is tilted up and surfaces within a few kilometres; the south beam is tilted down by the same angle, dives through the Earth, and re-emerges hundreds of kilometres away. A chord launched at depression angle θ from a sphere comes back up at a central angle of 2θ — so every milliradian of north-side tilt pushes the southern exit ~13 km farther down Illinois. Stratigraphy is a dipping, latitude-dependent model: unit tops are elevations interpolated between control columns at seven latitudes, anchored to NuMI/MINOS construction geology at the Fermilab end, ISGS drift and bedrock mapping (including the buried Mahomet Bedrock Valley) at the Champaign end, and the gas-storage fields for the deep mid-corridor — with a +60 m step at the Sandwich Fault Zone (41.55–41.62° N) carried explicitly. The strata fall away SE into the Illinois Basin, so the chord bottoms in the brackish Mt. Simon and exits through drift over Pennsylvanian shale, not through Fermilab's column. Mid-corridor tops are good to ±75–150 m — scale of tunnelling conditions and matter traversal, not a shaft siting; details and error bars in the aquifer audit. One ν interaction length at these energies is ~10¹¹ g/cm² — the whole Earth chord is a 10⁻⁴-level perturbation, so the beam arrives at the far exit essentially undiminished.

Rock traversed assumes a mean crustal density of 2.7 g/cm³. Absorption is negligible: even 400 km of rock is ~10⁸ g/cm², and a 3 TeV neutrino has an interaction probability under 0.1 % — the Earth is transparent to this beam, so it arrives at the far exit essentially undiminished. Exit points use a spherical Earth (R = 6371 km) with the surface held at the Fermilab-site elevation; real terrain along the path shifts the southern exit by roughly ±5 km per ±100 m of elevation difference, so treat it as a county-level answer, not a parcel-level one.
