Launch a beam, see where it comes out

This is a straight-line ray trace over the real elevation profile of the corridor. You set three things about the machine: where the collision point sits (N–S along the on-site corridor), the height (elevation) of the straightaway, and its upward tilt. The neutrino beam then travels in a perfectly straight line while the Earth curves away beneath it and the terrain rolls above it. The tool shows the whole story downstream: the beam's depth where it crosses the detector site (whose latitude you also choose), the point where it emerges from the ground, and how it sits relative to the airspace over private property north of Smith Road (41.935618 N).

Drag the ▲ collision point in 2-D to move the machine north–south and up–down; drag the ■ detector hall along the corridor; drag the ● emergence point along the ground to ask "what tilt puts the beam out here?" — the tilt slider updates to the required angle.

Reading the answer

  • The beam is one straight line, fixed entirely by the three machine inputs. Everything downstream — detector depth, emergence point, house clearance — is a consequence, not a choice. Dragging the emergence point just inverts the last step for you: it solves the tilt that would put the first ground crossing there (terrain bumps en route can still intercept the ray first — watch the minimum-cover readout).
  • Earth curvature (R = 6371 km) drops the ground away from the beam by s²/2R ≈ 7.8 m per 10 km: even a perfectly horizontal straightaway eventually surfaces. Heights here are measured from the curved geoid, which is why the straight beam plots as a gentle parabola.
  • The straight-section tilt equals the ring-plane pitch for a planar ring. LEP's plane was tilted 14 mrad (0.8°); values beyond ~15 mrad are flagged as beyond engineering precedent.
  • An upward-tilted north straight tilts the south-going plume of the same straight down by the same angle, pushing the southern exit farther away — see the safety assessment.
  • Once airborne the beam keeps climbing relative to the curving ground. Open-air showers need hundreds of metres of air to develop, so an overhead beam is far more benign than a surface-grazing one — but structures sitting in the core become shower media, so low overflight of dwellings still needs its own dose case (King 1999, §2).
