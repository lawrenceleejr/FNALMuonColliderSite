Launch a beam, see where it comes out

This is a straight-line ray trace over the real elevation profile of the corridor. You set three things about the machine: where the collision point sits (N–S along the on-site corridor), the height (elevation) of the straightaway, and its upward tilt. The neutrino beam then travels in a perfectly straight line while the Earth curves away beneath it and the terrain rolls above it. The tool shows the whole story downstream: the beam's depth where it crosses the detector (whose latitude you choose — anywhere from inside the Fermilab site to 13 km north), the point where it emerges from the ground, and how it sits relative to the airspace over private property north of Smith Road (41.935618 N).

Drag the ▲ collision point in 2-D to move the machine north–south and up–down; drag the ■ detector along the corridor; drag the ● emergence point along the ground to ask "what tilt puts the beam out here?"
Or work backwards: pick a constraint below, lock the machine parameters you want held fixed, and the remaining one solves itself to satisfy it.

Reading the answer

  • The beam is one straight line, fixed entirely by the three machine inputs. Everything downstream — detector depth, emergence point, airspace clearance — is a consequence, not a choice.
  • Locks invert the problem. With a constraint active, the first unlocked parameter in the order tilt → IP height → IP latitude becomes the solved one (marked solving) and is driven by bisection. Lock the tilt and the IP height solves instead; lock both and the IP latitude does. Lock all three and nothing can adapt, so the constraint is reported but not enforced.
  • Earth curvature (R = 6371 km) drops the ground away from the beam by s²/2R ≈ 7.8 m per 10 km: even a perfectly horizontal straightaway eventually surfaces. Heights follow the curved geoid, which is why the straight beam plots as a gentle parabola.
  • The straight-section tilt equals the ring-plane pitch for a planar ring. LEP's plane was tilted 14 mrad (0.8°); values beyond ~15 mrad are flagged as beyond engineering precedent. The wedge drawn at the IP is exaggerated by the plot's vertical scale — trust the number, not the angle you see.
  • An upward-tilted north straight tilts the south-going plume of the same straight down by the same angle, pushing the southern exit farther away — see the safety assessment.
  • Once airborne the beam keeps climbing relative to the curving ground. Open-air showers need hundreds of metres of air to develop, so an overhead beam is far more benign than a surface-grazing one — but structures sitting in the core become shower media, so low overflight of dwellings still needs its own dose case (King 1999, §2).
