Launch a beam, see where it comes out

This is a straight-line ray trace over the real elevation profile of the corridor. You set three things about the machine: where the collision point sits (N–S along the on-site corridor), the height (elevation) of the straightaway, and its upward tilt. The neutrino beam then travels in a perfectly straight line while the Earth curves away beneath it and the terrain rolls above it. The tool shows the whole story downstream: the beam's depth where it crosses the detector (whose latitude you choose — anywhere from inside the Fermilab site to 13 km north), the point where it emerges from the ground, how it sits relative to the airspace over private property north of Smith Road (41.935618 N), which rock units it tunnels through, and how many neutrino interaction lengths of matter each beam crosses on its way out of the Earth.

Drag the ▲ collision point in 2-D to move the machine north–south and up–down; drag the ■ detector along the corridor; drag the ● emergence point along the ground to ask "what tilt puts the beam out here?"
Or work backwards: pick a constraint below, lock the machine parameters you want held fixed, and the remaining one solves itself to satisfy it.

Where both beams leave the Earth

The straight section fires neutrinos both ways. The north beam is tilted up and surfaces within a few kilometres; the south beam is tilted down by the same angle, dives through the Earth, and re-emerges hundreds of kilometres away. A chord launched at depression angle θ from a sphere comes back up at a central angle of 2θ — so every milliradian of north-side tilt pushes the southern exit ~13 km farther down Illinois. Stratigraphy is a rough flat-layer model of northeastern Illinois (glacial drift over Silurian dolomite, Maquoketa shale, Galena–Platteville dolomite — the unit that hosts Fermilab's deep NuMI/MINOS halls — then the sandstone and dolomite section down to the Mt. Simon and Precambrian basement), with unit depths good to a few tens of percent; the strata actually dip gently south into the Illinois Basin. It is here to set the scale of tunnelling conditions and matter traversal, not to site a shaft. One ν interaction length at these energies is ~10¹¹ g/cm² — the whole Earth chord is a 10⁻⁴-level perturbation, so the beam arrives at the far exit essentially undiminished.

Rock traversed assumes a mean crustal density of 2.7 g/cm³. Absorption is negligible: even 400 km of rock is ~10⁸ g/cm², and a 3 TeV neutrino has an interaction probability under 0.1 % — the Earth is transparent to this beam, so it arrives at the far exit essentially undiminished. Exit points use a spherical Earth (R = 6371 km) with the surface held at the Fermilab-site elevation; real terrain along the path shifts the southern exit by roughly ±5 km per ±100 m of elevation difference, so treat it as a county-level answer, not a parcel-level one.

Reading the answer


    
  
  • The beam is one straight line, fixed entirely by the
  three machine inputs. Everything downstream — detector depth, emergence
  point, airspace clearance — is a consequence, not a choice.
    • 
  
  • Locks invert the problem. With a constraint active, the
  first unlocked parameter in the order tilt → IP height → IP latitude
  becomes the solved one (marked solving) and is
  driven by bisection. Lock the tilt and the IP height solves instead; lock
  both and the IP latitude does. Lock all three and nothing can adapt, so the
  constraint is reported but not enforced.
    • 
  
  • Earth curvature (R = 6371 km) drops the ground away
  from the beam by s²/2R ≈ 7.8 m per 10 km: even a perfectly horizontal
  straightaway eventually surfaces. Heights follow the curved geoid, which is
  why the straight beam plots as a gentle parabola.
    • 
  
  • The straight-section tilt equals the ring-plane pitch
  for a planar ring. LEP's plane was tilted 14 mrad (0.8°); values beyond
  ~15 mrad are flagged as beyond engineering precedent. The wedge drawn at the
  IP is exaggerated by the plot's vertical scale — trust the number, not the
  angle you see.
    • 
  
  • An upward-tilted north straight tilts the south-going plume of
  the same straight down by the same angle, pushing the southern
  exit farther away — see the safety assessment.
    • 
  
  • Geology is the flat-layer sketch drawn in the cross-section:
  glacial drift over the Silurian dolomite (the shallow-tunnel host), the
  Maquoketa shale, and the Galena–Platteville dolomite that hosts Fermilab's
  existing deep halls. Unit depths are rough (±30%-ish) and the strata dip
  gently south into the Illinois Basin, so read it as tunnelling context, not
  a site investigation.
    • 
  
  • Once airborne the beam keeps climbing relative to the curving ground.
  Open-air showers need hundreds of metres of air to develop, so an overhead
  beam is far more benign than a surface-grazing one — but structures sitting
  in the core become shower media, so low overflight of dwellings
  still needs its own dose case (King 1999, §2).
